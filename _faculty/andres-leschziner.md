The Leschziner laboratory uses cryo-electron microscopy and related structural approaches to study how macromolecular dynamics enable biological function, with research centered on three areas: cytoplasmic dynein and microtubule-based intracellular transport, chromatin remodeling complexes and nucleosome dynamics, and the Parkinson's Disease-linked kinase LRRK2. Chromatin remodeling ATPases mobilize nucleosomes to control genomic accessibility—a critical determinant of gene expression, DNA repair, and replication—and are frequently mutated in cancer. Dynein is the primary motor driving cargo transport toward microtubule minus ends in neurons, and defects contribute to neurodegenerative disease. Most recently, the lab has investigated LRRK2 structural biology to understand how its most common disease-causing mutations alter function, seeking new therapeutic vulnerabilities. The lab moved to the Department of Biochemistry and Biophysics at Weill Cornell Medicine in 2025.
