The **Tri-Institutional Chemical Biology Symposium** returned for its 21st year on August 28, 2025, bringing together scientists from across the community to highlight advances at the interface of chemistry and biology. Organized and hosted by students in the Tri-Institutional PhD Program in Chemical Biology (TPCB), the day-long event was held at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center and featured seminars by distinguished faculty speakers, presentations from TPCB students, and a lively poster session. More than 140 scientists participated in person, with the meeting also webcast to reach a wider audience.

The symposium opened with a keynote seminar by **Prof. Sichen Susan Shao** of Harvard Medical School and the Howard Hughes Medical Institute, who presented her lab’s work on the quality control systems that direct nascent proteins to the correct subcellular locations. Using inducible localization systems and site-specific UV photocrosslinking, her group uncovered a central role for the P5A-ATPase (SPF1) in ensuring that mitochondrial proteins avoid mistargeting to the endoplasmic reticulum. The first student presentation came from TPCB’s **Noah Yardeny**, who described his work with Prof. Jacob Geri at Weill Cornell to develop new iridium photocatalysts for proximity photolabeling of ligand–protein interactions. This µmap platform enabled proteome-wide determination of drug binding affinities, which could be extended to living cells and tissue homogenates.

Next, TPCB student **Nicole Walker** presented her research with Prof. Yael David at MSK on mechanisms of CAR-T cell exhaustion. She discovered that the glycolysis byproduct methylglyoxal drives histone glycation and exhaustion-related transcriptional programs in T cells. By engineering CAR-T cells that detoxify methylglyoxal, her work offers a strategy to enhance the durability and efficacy of CAR-T therapies. Closing the morning session, **Prof. Khalid Salaita** of Emory University shared his group’s pioneering efforts to visualize the biophysical forces that regulate T-cell receptor signaling. Using DNA-based molecular tension probes and a new covalent tagging strategy called Mechano-ID, his lab has quantified piconewton-scale forces at the TCR–pMHC interface and identified proteins recruited under tension, providing new insights into immune activation.

The morning talks were followed by a poster session that featured over 50 presentations from students, postdocs, and other scientists across the Tri-Institutional campuses and beyond. The presentations spanned topics from proteomics and RNA biology to chemical immunology and drug discovery. The posters were evaluated by the visiting keynote speakers and TPCB faculty members, and awardees were selected in four categories, with poster prizes generously provided our promotional partners.

The afternoon seminars began with a presentation from **Prof. Steve Bonilla** of Rockefeller University and TPCB, who spoke about his lab’s structural studies of viral RNAs. By combining cryo-electron microscopy with biophysical methods, his team uncovered conformational ensembles in dengue and other flavivirus RNAs that regulate genome replication initiation, revealing how viruses rely on conformationally dynamic RNA structures to regulate their lifecycles. This was followed by TPCB student **Tiffany Zhang** from Prof. Katya Vinogradova’s lab at Rockefeller, who introduced a multiomic platform for analyzing macrophage activation that integrates transcriptomics, proteomics, and cysteine-reactivity profiling. Her work identified state-dependent formation of a protein complex and revealed new functional mechanisms that link macrophage signaling to immune responses. In the final keynote address, **Prof. Alanna Schepartz** presented her group’s progress at UC Berkeley in the delivery of peptides and proteins into cells using mini-protein tags. This system harnesses pH-dependent unfolding and lipid interactions to enable efficient endosomal escape of cargo proteins for delivery to the cytosol and nucleus. They further demonstrated the successful replacement of a missing metabolic enzyme as proof of principle for an enzyme replacement therapy, highlighting the translational potential of these protein delivery systems.
